Ch 1 • Thinking Corner 1 (Page 3) Thinking Corner
When will $A \times B$ be equal to $B \times A$?
✍️ STEP-BY-STEP SOLUTION — Ch 1 • Thinking Corner 1 (Page 3) Cartesian Product Equality
Must-Use Formula / Concept In general, $A \times B \neq B \times A$ for two distinct non-empty sets. Equality holds if and only if $A = B$ or at least one of the sets is empty ($A = \phi$ or $B = \phi$).
Step 1
Recall the definition of Cartesian product: $A \times B = \{(a, b) \mid a \in A, b \in B\}$ and $B \times A = \{(b, a) \mid b \in B, a \in A\}$.
Step 2
For every ordered pair $(a, b) \in A \times B$ to also belong to $B \times A$, every element of $A$ must be in $B$ ($A \subseteq B$) and every element of $B$ must be in $A$ ($B \subseteq A$), which means $A = B$.
Step 3
Also, if either $A = \phi$ or $B = \phi$, then $A \times B = \phi$ and $B \times A = \phi$, so $A \times B = B \times A$ holds vacuously.
$A \times B = B \times A$ when $A = B$ (or when $A = \phi$ or $B = \phi$).
Condition for A × B = B × A

✍️ STEP-BY-STEP SOLUTION — Ch 1 • Progress Check 2 (Page 7) Testing Relations as Subsets
Must-Use Formula / Concept A relation $R$ from $A$ to $B$ must be a subset of $A \times B$ ($R \subseteq A \times B$). A relation from $B$ to $A$ must be a subset of $B \times A$ ($R \subseteq B \times A$).
Part 1 (i)
$\{(1, b), (1, c), (3, a), (4, b)\}$: Every first coordinate belongs to $A$ and every second coordinate belongs to $B$. Since it is a subset of $A \times B$, Yes, it is a relation from $A$ to $B$.
Part 1 (ii)-(iii)
• In 1(ii), $(b, 4) \notin A \times B$ because $b \notin A$. So Not a relation from $A$ to $B$.
• In 1(iii), $(a, 1), (b, 2) \notin A \times B$ because $a, b \notin A$. So Not a relation from $A$ to $B$.
Part 2 (i)
In 2(i) $\{(c, a), (c, b), (c, 1)\}$, the pairs $(c, a)$ and $(c, b)$ do not belong to $B \times A$ since $a, b \notin A$. So Not a relation from $B$ to $A$.
Part 2 (ii)-(iii)

• In 2(iii) $\{(a, 4), (b, 3), (c, 2)\} \subseteq B \times A$ $\implies$ Yes, it is a relation from $B$ to $A$.

Can there be a one to many function?
✍️ STEP-BY-STEP SOLUTION — Ch 1 • Thinking Corner 3 (Page 20) Uniqueness of Function Image
Must-Use Formula / Concept By definition of a function $f : A \to B$, each element $x \in A$ must have a unique image $f(x) \in B$.
Step 1
A 'one-to-many' correspondence means a single element in the domain $A$ is mapped to two or more distinct elements in the co-domain $B$.
Step 2
However, the fundamental definition of a function requires that every input has only one unique output.
Step 3
Therefore, a one-to-many correspondence can be a relation, but it can never be a function.
No, there cannot be a one-to-many function because every element in the domain of a function must have a unique image.

✍️ STEP-BY-STEP SOLUTION — Ch 1 • Thinking Corner 4 (Page 23) Identity Function Property
Must-Use Formula / Concept An identity function $I_A : A \to A$ is defined by $f(x) = x$ for all $x \in A$.

Must-Use Formula / Concept For finite sets $A$ and $B$: (i) One-one exists $\iff n(A) \le n(B)$; (ii) Onto exists $\iff n(A) \ge n(B)$; (iii) Bijection exists $\implies n(A) = n(B)$.

✍️ STEP-BY-STEP SOLUTION — Ch 1 • Thinking Corner 5 (Page 26) Commutativity of Power Functions
Must-Use Formula / Concept Use the law of exponents $(x^a)^b = x^{ab}$ to evaluate $(f \circ g)(x)$ and $(g \circ f)(x)$.
Step 1
Compute $(f \circ g)(x)$:
$(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x^n) = (x^n)^m = x^{nm} = x^{mn}$.
Step 2
Compute $(g \circ f)(x)$:
$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(x^m) = (x^m)^n = x^{mn}$.
Step 3
Since $x^{mn} = x^{nm}$ for all $x$, we get $(f \circ g)(x) = (g \circ f)(x)$.
Yes, $f \circ g = g \circ f = x^{mn}$.
